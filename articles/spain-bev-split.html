Spain's BEV Transition Is Two Stories — and the Gap Is Wider Than Italy's

The promised sequel: split the registrations and Spain's private market runs at Korea speed while the rental fleets stand still

By @LeRaffl · Data: DGT

When I wrote up Italy's rental split last month, I ended with a prediction I couldn't check: "Spain is the obvious next place to look — comparable tourism, comparable geography, quite possibly the same hidden split. I don't have the data on that yet." Now I have the data, and it's better data than I hoped for. Spain's traffic authority DGT publishes its monthly registrations as raw microdata: one record per registered vehicle, with the registration channel flagged on every single one[1]. No summary PDFs, no estimation, no backfilling. Whatever split we make is exact by construction.

Before taking anything apart, though, let's establish what the headline view of Spain actually looks like. The Gallery draws four standard charts for every country; here they are for Spain, whole market, latest data.

The state of play — four charts, one country

Spain, all registrations: BEV at just under 10% of the trailing twelve months.

First, the trajectory. Spain's BEV share sits just under 10% on a trailing-twelve-month basis, and the fitted curve implies roughly 15–16 years to climb from 20% to 80% BEV share. That's mid-table: clearly ahead of Italy's ~24 years, nowhere near the leaders. Fittingly, when the Italy article called the Italian non-rental market "Spain / South Korea territory", whole-market Spain was the benchmark for ordinary.

The transition timers: BEV rising 20%→80%, ICE falling 80%→20%.
ICE falling steadily — but the BEV curve isn't rising at the same rate. Something in between is absorbing the difference.
The trailing-twelve-month powertrain split. The big band in the middle is hybrids.

And there it is, in the powertrain split: the thing absorbing Spain's combustion decline is not the battery car, it's the hybrid. Non-plug-in hybrids alone are 43.5% of the last twelve months — add PHEV's 12% and more than half of everything registered in Spain now carries some kind of electric motor, while pure BEV is stuck under 10%. Petrol is down to 24%, diesel to under 6%.

The way this usually gets narrated, in Spanish and international coverage alike, is a story about obstacles: charging that's too sparse and too slow, sticker prices that are too high, subsidy programs strangled by bureaucracy, and a general suspicion that Spain — a country of apartment dwellers, long distances and rental cars — just isn't built for BEVs, so hybrids are the sensible compromise[2]. It's not a crazy story. But two things in these four charts don't fit it. First, the hybrid share: 43% is not what "buyers held back by obstacles" looks like — it's what a market in the middle of a very specific migration looks like, and it's worth asking what's driving it. Second, the trajectory is visibly bending upward right now — the monthly BEV share hit 10.8% in May 2026. If infrastructure and prices were the binding constraint for everyone, who exactly is buying all these BEVs?

So let's take the market apart. Same move as Italy: split every registration by channel — rental fleets versus everything else.

The split

A quick word on definitions. In the DGT microdata, my Rental segment is every registration flagged as rent-a-car service plus every registration flagged as renting/leasing — together 36% of the Spanish market over the last twelve months, even more weight than the roughly-a-third rental carries in Italy. NonRental is the exact complement: mostly private buyers, plus direct corporate purchases. Because the flags sit on each individual vehicle record, Rental + NonRental = Whole to the last unit[1].

And the rental channel alone:

In Italy, the split turned a laggard into an ordinary market. In Spain, it turns an ordinary market into a fast one. Strip out the rental fleets and the 20%→80% timer drops from ~15.6 years to ~11.5 years, with the 20% line crossed around 2028. For calibration: South Korea's entire market — one of the benchmarks for "moving briskly" — runs a ~17.5-year timer on current data. Spain's non-rental buyers are outpacing it. (Not a perfectly fair comparison, a sub-segment against a whole market, but that's exactly the point: Spain's headline number is a blend, and the blend hides this.)

The distance between the two Spains is remarkable. Over the last twelve months, non-rental BEV share is 13.0%; rental is 4.3% — a factor of three, where Italy's gap is closer to one and a half. And it's widening: in May 2026 the monthly figures were 16.5% versus 3.1%. Five to one. Italy's split was two speeds. Spain's is two different decades.

Why the rental fleets specifically

The mechanism is the one from the Italy piece, so I'll compress it: a private buyer opts in — they researched the car, they know where they'll charge. A rental customer opts into nothing. They land at an airport in a country whose charging network they don't know, get handed a car they didn't choose, and drive off. The car has to just work from minute one, and if it doesn't, the complaint lands on the rental company. So the rental company buys whatever cannot generate that complaint. In Spain that pressure is enormous, because Spain isn't just a tourism country — it's the second-most-visited country on Earth, and its rent-a-car fleet is one of Europe's largest.

You can watch this logic execute in the monthly data, almost like a lab experiment. Spanish rental registrations are fiercely seasonal: the fleets stock up in spring for the summer season — March is the biggest month of the year, with 62,000 rental registrations in March 2026 against 14,000 in sleepy August 2025. And precisely when the volume peaks, the rental BEV share collapses: 6.5% in quiet January 2026, then 4.0% in February, 2.8% in March, 2.6% in April. Meanwhile the hybrid share of those same spring purchases hit 48%. Same pattern the year before. Read that again: when Spain's rental fleets bought their 2026 tourist-season cars, they chose hybrids almost ten times as often as BEVs. That single procurement decision, repeated every spring across a third of the market, is a huge part of why Spain's headline number looks mediocre.

The charging picture: two networks, two verdicts

"But the charging!" — yes, let's talk about the charging, because it also splits in two, and the two halves map exactly onto the two Spains.

The public network is the tourists' network, and it is genuinely the weak flank. Spain counted around 55,000 operational public charging points at the end of Q1 2026[3], having passed 50,000 around New Year[4] — growing double-digit percent per year, with the fast segment growing fastest: points in the 50–250 kW class more than doubled during 2025, and points above 250 kW nearly did[4]. That's real momentum. But the density is still below where the EU wants it, a large share of the installed base is slow AC, and the network clusters where Spaniards live — Catalonia, Madrid, Andalusia — rather than along every route a tourist might improvise. A visitor in a rented BEV faces the Italy problem: they can't know in advance whether their particular corner of Spain is the well-served kind, and one stranding writes their opinion of EVs permanently.

The private side is a different world, and it's the part the "Spain is a country of apartments" objection consistently misses. Since 2014, Spanish electrotechnical regulation (ITC-BT-52) has required new buildings with parking to include pre-installation for vehicle charging[5]. Under the horizontal property law, an apartment owner who wants a wallbox on their own parking space doesn't need the homeowner association's permission — they only have to notify it[6]. The outgoing MOVES III program subsidised home charger installations at up to 70–80% of cost right through the end of 2025[7]. Spaniards without any parking space exist, plenty of them — but the addressable population with a garage or a workplace socket is far larger than the objection implies, and for them charging is a solved problem.

And here Spain holds a card almost nobody in this debate plays: the sun. Solar photovoltaics delivered a record 19.1% of peninsular generation in 2025 — 27.1% in June, the highest monthly share ever recorded — and Spain keeps installing at record pace[8]. On sunny days, midday wholesale electricity prices routinely fall to nearly zero, which has made Spanish household electricity among the cheapest in Western Europe[9]. A car that charges at home overnight or at work at noon plugs straight into that. Spain is arguably the best place in Europe to own a car you plug in — provided you have somewhere to plug it. Which is precisely the line that separates the resident from the tourist, the non-rental curve from the rental curve. The infrastructure objection isn't wrong. It's just aimed at the wrong segment.

From diesel country to hybrid country

That leaves the first anomaly: the 43% hybrid share, the highest of any market I chart. Where does that come from? History, mostly. A decade ago Spain was a diesel country — 63% of new registrations in 2015 were diesel. Then diesel's reputation and resale values collapsed, and within ten years its share fell to under 6%. Two-thirds of the market had to migrate somewhere.

For a buyer burned once by betting on the "wrong" technology, the full hybrid is a seductive landing spot: no plug, no new habits, no range questions, a familiar brand promising familiar reliability with a green sticker on top. It is genuinely practical — that's exactly why it's dangerous. The migration went diesel → hybrid, not diesel → BEV, and it went that way in every channel: hybrids sit at 43–44% among private buyers and rental fleets alike.

Non-rental powertrain split: hybrids dominate here too — but BEV and PHEV are eating in fast.
Rental powertrain split: the same hybrid wall, with BEV barely visible.

This is the real counterweight to Spain's acceleration — not the charging post. A 43%-of-market comfort zone that feels like progress can delay the actual switch for years; the bridge becomes the destination. If there's one number that decides which of Spain's two stories wins, it's whether the hybrid share has peaked. The early evidence from the buyers who choose freely is encouraging: among non-rental registrations, plug-in share (BEV plus PHEV) went from 16% to 27% in a single year. Where people opt in, the plug is winning ground quickly. The hybrid wall is being eaten from the private side.

The trap, drawn out

You don't actually need Spain's data to see how a hybrid trap forms — a back-of-envelope model is enough, and I sketched this one out a while ago. Picture a market that starts 100% combustion. Every year a fixed slice of ICE owners switch to a hybrid, a smaller slice jump straight to a BEV, and a slice of the people already in hybrids finally make the jump to a BEV. Three numbers, nothing else, applied year after year.

The trap lives in the gap between two of those numbers. If people move from combustion to hybrid faster than they move from hybrid to BEV, hybrids pile up in the middle — a bulge that can dominate the market for a decade before it drains. The one that decides everything is Hybrid → BEV: choke it and the green BEV band is held back for years behind a fat amber wall; open it and the wall dissolves and the market flips to BEV in a handful of years.

Spain is living in the fat part of that amber band right now: hybrids near their peak at 43%, plug-ins climbing underneath. Which way the next few years break comes down to exactly the rate the model makes you feel — how quickly today's hybrid buyer comes back for a BEV next time. And that's the useful reframe, because almost everything in the next section is really a lever on that one transition rate.

The levers Italy doesn't have

Italy's article ended in frustration: the fixes existed, but nobody seemed to be reaching for them. Spain's position is materially better, for three reasons that hardly ever make it into the standard narrative.

  • Plan Auto+ launches this month. MOVES III, the old subsidy scheme, was notorious for its bureaucracy — buyers fronted the full price and waited, sometimes years, for reimbursement from regional governments. Its successor, announced in December 2025 under the national Auto 2030 strategy[10], flips the model: the discount is applied by the dealer at the moment of purchase, funded centrally, retroactive to January 1, 2026, with the rules due in the official gazette in early July[11]. Up to €4,500 for a private BEV buyer without scrapping, up to €7,000 with a scrapped old car, on cars up to €45,000 before tax — and dealers must add at least €1,000 of their own[12]. One honest caveat: the retroactivity means a large chunk of the €400 million budget is reportedly committed before the window even opens, and the pot may run dry in autumn[13]. Still — a purchase incentive that works at the till, in the same year the non-rental market is already accelerating on its own, is fuel on a fire that's already burning.
  • The solar dividend. Covered above, but it belongs in this list: cheap midday electricity is a structural, permanent, widening advantage that no Northern European market can copy, and it compounds with every wallbox installed.
  • A domestic car industry with skin in the game. Spain is Europe's second-largest car producer. Small electric cars are now rolling off Spanish lines — SEAT/Cupra in Martorell among them — and the Auto 2030 plan explicitly targets an "affordable Spanish electric car"[10]. Italy's transition threatens its tourism industry; Spain's transition, done right, feeds its manufacturing industry. The political economy points the other way here.

The Italy checklist, applied

The Italy article closed with four portable rules. Spain is the first market I get to test them on, so let's score it honestly.

  • "Find the drivers who didn't opt in — they're an early-warning indicator." Found them: 36% of the market, frozen on a fifty-year curve, buying hybrids by the tens of thousands every spring. The indicator is flashing.
  • "For a tourism economy, prioritise DC coverage where people drive, not where your population lives." Half-happening. Fast charging is the fastest-growing segment of the network, but deployment still follows Spanish residents rather than tourist corridors and islands. Nothing in the current policy mix targets this directly.
  • "Cap ad-hoc prices." Not happening. A tourist without a subscription app still faces surprise pricing at the post. Same gap as Italy.
  • "Build the network before scaling purchase incentives or mandates." Spain is running the experiment in the opposite order: Auto+ scales purchase incentives now, while the tourist-facing network still lags. For the private market that's the right call — their charging is solved at home, the subsidy just accelerates what's already moving. For the rental third, Auto+ changes nothing: no fleet quota, no corridor build-out, no pricing rule. The frozen curve stays frozen by design.

Score: two out of four, with the two misses concentrated exactly where Spain's problem segment is. Spain has, probably by accident, built a policy mix that's excellent for the market that was already fast and irrelevant for the market that's stuck.

What happens next

So Spain is not "a slow-ish BEV market with charging problems". It's a fast private market and a frozen rental market wearing a single trend line, with a forty-percent hybrid cushion muffling the whole thing. The blended headline number will keep looking mediocre for a while — that's arithmetic, as long as a third of the market buys tourist hybrids every spring.

Three things to watch over the next 12–18 months, all of them visible in these exact charts as the Gallery updates monthly. First: does Plan Auto+ push the non-rental monthly BEV share past 20%? It was at 16.5% in May, before a single euro of the new scheme was paid out; the fitted curve says 2028, and I wouldn't be surprised to see it earlier. Second: does anything unfreeze the rental curve — a big fleet order, a quota, a corridor program? Until something does, treat every "Spain is slow" headline as a statement about rental procurement, not about Spaniards. Third: does the hybrid share finally peak? That's the number the whole transition hinges on.

Italy's split ended with "the people are fine — worry about your tourism." Spain's version is better: the people are not just fine, they're fast, the sun is subsidising them daily, and the cavalry of cheap Spanish-built EVs plus at-the-till discounts is arriving this very month. Now give the tourists somewhere to plug in, and Spain's two stories can finally become one — the good one.
